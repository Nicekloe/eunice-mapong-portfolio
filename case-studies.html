Making 200 consultant profiles answerable to what RFPs actually ask

LGS, an IBM company · Internal initiative
IT Consultant — Business Analysis · 2025

The situation

Responding to a government RFP meant finding the right consultants and adapting their CVs by hand. Every response started more or less from scratch: profiles were reviewed manually, reshaped for that tender, and the same judgment calls were made again the next time. It was slow at exactly the moment speed mattered, because tender windows don't move.

What was asked for, and what was actually needed

The initiative was framed around introducing AI to accelerate matching between RFP requirements and available consultants. But a matching system can only be as good as what it matches against, and the profiles it would read weren't written to be read that way — some 200 CVs in different shapes, describing the same skills in different words. Before any automation could help, two things had to exist: a shared vocabulary for describing a consultant, and an explicit statement of what counts as a match. Both were business judgment calls, not model behaviour.

Finding the real problem

Rather than starting from what consultants had written about themselves, the analysis started from the other end — what government tenders actually ask for. Reading across a body of RFPs surfaced the recurring patterns: which IT roles come up, which skills and technologies get named, what experience levels are specified, and which criteria decide the outcome.

It also surfaced a consistent mismatch. Tenders expected a profile to show the tasks a consultant had performed — described to a particular pattern — the deliverables they had produced, and the methodologies they had worked in. Consultant CVs frequently didn't carry those things at all, and where they did, they weren't framed the way evaluators expected to read them.

So every response involved re-shaping the same material into the same expected form, under deadline, by whoever happened to be assembling the bid. The bottleneck wasn't effort — it was that nothing upstream had ever been written to the buyer's format.

Making the rules explicit

Those findings were consolidated with the team into an internal reference framework, and validation workshops settled the parts that needed agreement rather than analysis: how a profile should be described, and the rules by which an RFP requirement and a consultant's capability count as a match. The business rules, functional requirements and framework direction were documented, so updating a CV became a defined activity rather than an individual interpretation.

Adoption was treated as part of the work rather than an afterthought — training and communication material was produced so the framework would actually be used, and 200+ consultant profiles were standardised against it.

~50%less time to select and update CVs
200+consultant profiles standardised

Profiles now describe the same skill the same way and can be matched against a tender's stated criteria instead of read one by one — and the people assembling bids against a deadline no longer rebuild each profile into the expected shape from scratch.

Bringing incident and change management up to ITIL 4

Sécurité Électronique (SECEL) Sarl · Client: a multi-site organisation
Business/Functional Analyst · 2020

The situation

End users across several sites were waiting too long on incidents and change requests. Support teams weren't consistently following the processes that existed, and some of those processes had fallen out of alignment with ITIL 4.

What was asked for, and what was actually needed

The mandate was to bring incident and change management into line with ITIL 4. The prior question was what the teams were actually doing, because the documented processes and the practised ones had diverged — and rewriting a process people were already working around would have produced a better-documented version of the same problem.

Establishing the real starting point

A requirements workshop with business and IT stakeholders set the objectives and priorities. An AS-IS analysis then established what was actually happening rather than what the process documents claimed, surfacing the operational irritants, the points where practice had drifted from ITIL 4, the governance risks created by unclear ownership, and the places where different sites solved the same problem differently.

More than ten improvement recommendations came out of that analysis, all of them accepted by the client and translated into an optimisation roadmap. The TO-BE processes were modelled to make explicit what the old ones left implicit: who does what, at which step, with which control points, and which indicators show whether it's working. Validation workshops walked stakeholders through the target processes, which were approved without major modification, and the processes were then presented to their owners — so the people accountable for running them had seen them before inheriting them.

10+recommendations, all accepted

The mandate ended with the roadmap accepted and the client committed to implementing it, so the improvement in resolution times isn't mine to claim. What the work did produce: every recommendation accepted, and target processes approved without major modification by the people who would own them.

A four-year IT roadmap a city actually adopted

Sécurité Électronique (SECEL) Sarl · Client: Communauté Urbaine de Douala, Cameroon
Business/Functional Analyst

The situation

A city administration needed to know what its information system actually consisted of, and what to do about it. The question spanned everything at once — applications, infrastructure, network, databases, security, IT governance and the processes running across them — and the infrastructure in particular had aged past the point where incremental fixes made sense.

What was asked for, and what was actually needed

The mandate was a strategic assessment and a target vision. But an assessment that ends in a diagnosis changes nothing. What the organisation needed was a roadmap it could fund — investments sequenced over four years, each traceable to a finding rather than to a preference, and concrete enough that someone could start on Monday. That set the standard the work had to meet: not “here is what's wrong”, but “here is what to do, in what order, and why this before that”.

Establishing what was really there

Audit plans were prepared and the engagement with roughly 80 stakeholders was coordinated and scheduled — interviews, questionnaires, direct observation and document analysis, deliberately in combination.

The combination is the point. An information system as described in documents, as described by the people who run it, and as observed in practice are three different systems. My role included analysing and validating the AS-IS models against what the fieldwork actually found — so the gap analysis that followed measured the distance between the real current state and the target state, not between an idealised current state and the target.

Turning findings into fundable decisions

More than 100 project deliverables were consolidated — framing notes, audit reports, execution plans, synthesis documents — into something an executive could decide from. Around 100 strategic project recommendations were documented, each intended to be acted on rather than noted. All of them were retained by the organisation.

~100recommendations, all retained
~80stakeholders engaged
4 yearsof investment sequenced

The roadmap was adopted. It became the basis for prioritising IT investment and for launching modernisation work across infrastructure, applications, data, security and governance — so the recommendations turned into funded projects rather than a report on a shelf.
