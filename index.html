Approach

Three things follow from that one commitment

Requirement lifecycle ownership isn't a list of activities I happen to perform. It's one position, and everything below is a consequence of holding it.

01

The stated requirement is rarely the real one

“Automate the process” or “modernise the platform” describes a symptom. Finding what sits underneath means going past what stakeholders say in a workshop — observing the work as it's actually done, reading the artefacts people produce, and noticing the gap between the process as described and the process as practised.

02

A development background changes what a spec can account for

Knowing how systems are built means functional specifications can address how each deliverable has to behave — dependencies between features, what an endpoint returns, which legacy behaviours must survive a migration untouched — at the analyst's altitude, without pretending to the architect's.

03

A requirement isn't done at handoff

Staying involved through UAT design, go-live and early life is where the gap between what was specified and what was actually built becomes visible. Sometimes that means recognising a reported bug as a requirements defect. Sometimes it means catching, in production, a limit nobody specified. Either way, being there is the point.

Keeping 25,000 users' behaviour intact through a cloud migration

LGS, an IBM company · Client: a Canadian insurance carrier
IT Consultant — Business Analysis · 2023–present

The situation

A digital transformation project was replacing a legacy VB.NET insurance application with a cloud-native web platform on Microsoft Azure. More than 25,000 agents and brokers depended on the old system. It processed requests slowly, cost a great deal to maintain, and — because it was installed workstation by workstation — couldn't be reached remotely.

What was asked for, and what was actually needed

The project was framed as a modernisation: better performance, accessibility, maintainability and operational continuity.

The harder requirement sat underneath it. During insurance product illustrations, the legacy application returned specific figures and responses to the agent mid-quotation, and those behaviours had to survive the move exactly — a migration that hit every stated objective while changing what an agent saw on screen would have failed. So the real work wasn't specifying the new platform. It was establishing precisely what the old one did, including behaviour that had never been written down and existed only in code that was being retired.

Finding the real problem

Over 100 user stories were analysed and clarified to surface the business rules, acceptance criteria, cross-feature dependencies and critical behaviours a safe migration depended on. That analysis routinely turned up ambiguities, inconsistencies and non-conformities in stories already accepted into the backlog — each one a defect that would otherwise have been discovered in development or, worse, after release.

One example shows the pattern. Analysing a single user story surfaced a dependency on an API that hadn't been finished yet. Nothing in the story said so; the dependency only became visible once the story's functional behaviour was traced through to the endpoints it would call. Left alone, the story would have entered a sprint that couldn't complete it. Raised early, it triggered a re-prioritisation and direct coordination with the team responsible for the API — and the sequencing problem was solved before it cost anyone a sprint.

Making the legacy behaviour durable

The existing system's flows, interactions and sequences were modelled explicitly, so the behaviours to be preserved existed as documentation rather than as tribal knowledge or as a binary nobody wanted to open. Functional documentation was maintained using a documentation-as-code (Markdown) approach, keeping requirements, decisions and system behaviour consistent across delivery teams as the platform changed underneath them.

Working with business analysts, developers, the solution architect and the Product Owner, functional needs were translated into implementable components aligned to the target architecture — and completed user stories were walked through with the delivery teams to confirm everyone was building against the same understanding.

Protecting the outcome

Ambiguity was treated as something to remove before it reached code, not something to discover during testing. Every user story assigned to me went through the same sequence: a short kick-off for that story alone with the subject-matter expert or customer-side business analyst who could actually answer for it, to pin down what the story meant and what its acceptance criteria had to be; an update to the story reflecting what that conversation settled; and a check that it still served the sprint goal, which in turn traced back to a business requirement. Only then did implementation start — in my case including the development work itself.

Around that: backlog prioritisation, estimation and refinement supported by analysis of business value, functional complexity and delivery dependencies; API and microservice behaviour validated with Postman; validation workshops and UAT attended and documented, with discrepancies tracked through to resolution.

After go-live

Involvement continued past deployment: tracking defects raised in production through to resolution, and making sure the team as a whole handled them properly rather than letting fixes happen ad hoc.

Turning a ten-day paper process into a two-hour one

Sécurité Électronique (SECEL) Sarl · Client: an international child-welfare NGO
Business/Functional Analyst · 2018

The situation

The NGO ran an academic excellence award programme across its associative homes. Eligibility combined two things: a student's membership in one of the homes, and their grades in subjects selected and pre-defined by the NGO. The whole process lived on paper and in Excel. Producing the eligibility lists and rankings took the award committee more than ten days.

What was asked for, and what was actually needed

The request was to automate the award process.

The rules that process depended on turned out not to exist in any single agreed form. Some were written in files; others were carried verbally by the people who had always run the programme. Automating that as-is would have hard-coded whichever version happened to get captured. The first deliverable wasn't software — it was an agreed statement of the rules themselves.

Finding the real problem

Elicitation combined workshops and interviews with direct observation of users doing the work, and analysis of the paper and Excel artefacts they produced.

Observation caught what the interviews hadn't described: the same student information was being recorded in several different files, updated by hand whenever something changed, with no version control over any of it. Nobody had raised this as a problem — it was simply how the work was done. But it meant information was regularly omitted, lost, or quietly ignored, and it explained why producing a defensible ranking took ten days.

The stated problem was slowness. The real problem was that no one could tell which copy of a student's record was the true one.

Making the rules durable

The eligibility rules, data sources, validation steps and ranking criteria were translated into functional requirements, business rules and acceptance criteria — each traceable forward into a test case and a validation. The functional file, user stories and detailed specifications carried that traceability end to end. Business processes were modelled in UML to make system interactions explicit, and the functional architecture was designed alongside the technical team so that what was specified and what got built stayed the same thing.

Protecting the outcome

A test plan of functional and integration cases was structured against the acceptance criteria, and UAT was coordinated end to end, with results, anomalies and corrections tracked to closure.

One of the anomalies stood out more. The client logged a defect: the generated award certificates didn't show complete student details. Investigating it showed the cause wasn't in the code — the requirements for the generated PDF award documents had never been captured completely. That made it a requirements defect, not a bug, and the two are fixed differently.

I raised this, brought the stakeholders together, and we worked through what the correction actually involved: what it affected, how it should be prioritised against the rest of the backlog, and how it would be introduced in the coming sprint.

Three end users were then trained through demonstrations and written material, so the system was handed to people who could operate it rather than to a deployment date.

After go-live

Support continued through the first live award cycle, and real use surfaced something no test had. Past a certain volume of student records entered in a single session, the system slowed noticeably. The symptoms showed up as operational workarounds before they showed up as a ticket — users waiting to register records, and the number of people connected at once being reduced to keep things moving. Investigation traced the cause to the architecture rather than to how the system was being used, which meant no amount of tuning or user guidance would fix it.

That finding was scoped into a second phase of the project. It also changed how I elicit: expected volumes, concurrency and performance thresholds are now drawn out explicitly rather than left to emerge in production.

10 days → 2 hoursto produce results and rankings

The awards committee worked from one reliable source of student data instead of reconciling several uncontrolled copies of it — and every eligible student's ranking could be traced back to the inputs that produced it, so the committee could see why a student placed where they did rather than take the list on trust.
